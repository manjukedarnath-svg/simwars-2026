Topics to Know — Self-Study Curriculum

This is a deliberately broad, self-study curriculum — wider than any single SimWars case. It's designed so you arrive prepared across the full spectrum of paediatric emergencies, without the specific scenarios being guessable in advance. Work through the tiers below in order.

Tier 1Must Know — The Non-Negotiable Fundamentals▼

These are the absolute pillars of paediatric resuscitation. A lapse here triggers automatic safety flags or critical zeros.

1. Core Resuscitation Mechanics (BLS/PALS)
  • High-Quality CPR Metrics: Exact rate (100–120/min), depth (≥5 cm or 1/3 AP diameter), full chest recoil, and minimising peri-shock and compression pauses.
  • Defibrillation vs. Cardioversion: Absolute differentiation between unsynchronized (for pulseless/polymorphic rhythms) and synchronized (for organised tachyarrhythmias with a pulse). Correct energy selection (2 J/kg for defib; 0.5–1 J/kg for sync cardioversion).
  • Vascular Access: Rapid IO placement in peri-arrest; recognising the IO as the preferred route during active CPR when IV access is delayed.
2. Airway & RSI Physiology (The Big Three Contraindications)
  • Agent Selection Based on Haemodynamics: Knowledge that Propofol and Midazolam cause profound vasodilation/myocardial depression and are contraindicated in shock states, low ejection fraction, or raised intracranial pressure (ICP).
  • Agent Selection Based on Pulmonary Mechanics: Ketamine is the bronchodilator of choice in reactive airway disease; Succinylcholine is absolutely contraindicated in hyperkalaemia or rhabdomyolysis (risk of fatal potassium release). Rocuronium is the safe alternative.
  • The "Physiologically Difficult Airway": The understanding that peri-intubation arrest is driven by pre-existing zero-reserve physiology (e.g., severe metabolic acidosis, shock) — thus requiring pre-emptive vasopressor (push-dose) preparation and haemodynamic optimisation before laryngoscopy.
3. Metabolic & Electrolyte Emergencies (The 4Ts & 4Hs)
  • Hyperkalaemia: The hierarchy — Calcium gluconate (membrane stabilisation) is the first and most critical intervention, followed by Bicarb, Insulin/Dextrose, and Salbutamol.
  • Hypoglycaemia: Immediate correction (Dextrose 10% at 2 mL/kg) within the first 60–120 seconds of arrival; recognising it as a common cause of refractory seizure or cardiac arrest.
  • Hyponatraemia (Symptomatic): Differentiating sinus tachycardia with widened QRS from Ventricular Tachycardia; correcting with 3% Hypertonic Saline while avoiding overly rapid correction (>8 mEq/L per 24h).
4. Shock Differentiation & Fluid Resuscitation
  • The Four Types of Shock: Hypovolaemic, Distributive (Septic/Anaphylactic), Cardiogenic, and Obstructive.
  • Fluid Responsiveness: Recognising hepatomegaly, gallop rhythm, and pulmonary oedema as signs of pump failure (cardiogenic) — fluid boluses are harmful here.
  • Obstructive Shock: Recognising raised intra-thoracic or intra-abdominal pressure as mechanical impediments to venous return — fluid is ineffective and detrimental.
Tier 2Critical Clinical Management — Good to Know▼

Scored as high-yield critical interventions — delays or omissions cost significant points. Separates top performers.

1. Specific Reversible Arrest Aetiologies
  • Polymorphic Ventricular Tachyarrhythmias: Recognition of prolonged QTc and the absolute requirement for Magnesium sulphate as the disease-modifying intervention. Strict avoidance of QT-prolonging drugs (e.g., Amiodarone, Ondansetron, Macrolides).
  • Environmental Emergencies (Temperature Extremes): For hypothermic arrest, the principle of "not dead until warm and dead" — resuscitation continues despite fixed/dilated pupils; defibrillation is the first action (not adrenaline); active multi-modality rewarming. For hyperthermia, aggressive cooling and seizure management.
  • Traumatic/Haemorrhagic Shock: Blood-first resuscitation (packed cells) over crystalloid; administration of Tranexamic Acid (TXA); activation of Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP); understanding the risks of crystalloid-induced dilutional coagulopathy.
2. Neurological Emergencies
  • Raised Intracranial Pressure (ICP): Recognition of Cushing's Triad (hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respirations) or pupillary asymmetry. Immediate ICP reduction bundle: head elevation >30°, neutral neck, normocapnia, and osmotherapy (3% Hypertonic Saline or Mannitol).
  • Status Epilepticus: Progression through benzodiazepines (Midazolam/Lorazepam) to second-line agents (Phenytoin, Valproate, or Levetiracetam) — recognising when standard doses fail and escalating to anaesthetic infusions.
  • Acute Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI): Maintaining cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) by avoiding hypotension and hypoxia; knowing when to repeat imaging on clinical deterioration.
3. Post-Intubation Deterioration (The DOPE Mnemonic)
  • Displacement, Obstruction, Pneumothorax, Equipment failure: Specifically, the clinical recognition of Tension Pneumothorax (decreased breath sounds, tracheal deviation, rising peak pressures, dropping SpO2). Needle decompression must occur before chest X-ray confirmation in a deteriorating patient. Absolute requirement for correct side and landmark (2nd intercostal space, mid-clavicular line).
4. Advanced Ventilator & Haemodynamic Mechanics
  • Ventilator-Associated Lung Injury: Understanding the PEEP-PVR-RV Afterload chain (high PEEP worsens right ventricular failure). Strategies include permissive hypercapnia, reducing Mean Airway Pressure (MAP), and prone positioning.
  • Capillary Leak Syndromes: Distinguishing intravascular depletion from third-spacing/ascites; measuring intra-abdominal pressure (IAP); halting crystalloid when mechanical compartment syndrome (abdominal or thoracic) is suspected.
5. Sepsis & Adrenal Insufficiency
  • Paediatric Septic Shock: The "golden hour" antibiotics (Ceftriaxone, Vancomycin, etc.) and the role of Hydrocortisone in refractory shock (absolute or relative adrenal insufficiency).
  • Anaphylaxis: Immediate IM Adrenaline as the first-line therapy; distinguishing it from distributive sepsis.
Tier 3Excellent to Know — Exceptional Performance▼

These topics score the extra marks in the "exceptional performance" range for top-tier distinction.

1. Advanced Investigative Interpretation
  • ECG Morphology: Identification of Osborn (J) waves in hypothermia; differentiating Torsades from monomorphic VT; identifying RV strain patterns (e.g., S1Q3T3, right axis deviation) in massive PE.
  • Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS): Interpretation of the D-sign (RV pressure overload), reduced TAPSE, cardiac contractility (EF%), and presence of pericardial effusion/tamponade. eFAST for free fluid.
  • Laboratory Anticipation: Proactively calculating electrolyte correction rates, stating weight-based doses (mg/kg) aloud without hesitation, and recognising signs of rhabdomyolysis (myoglobinuria, "cola-coloured" urine).
2. Definitive & Salvage Therapies
  • Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO): Defensible mode selection — understanding that V-V ECMO corrects hypoxaemia/hypercarbia to unload the RV, while V-A ECMO is reserved for refractory shock/cardiac arrest. Justifying the rationale aloud.
  • Surgical Source Control: Making the explicit decision to bypass CT and go directly to theatre for a positive FAST in an unstable haemorrhagic patient, versus using CT for stable patients.
  • Decompressive Procedures: Formal request for therapeutic paracentesis in abdominal compartment syndrome; chest drain insertion post-decompression.
3. Comprehensive Neuroprotection & Post-Resuscitation Care
  • Targeted Temperature Management (TTM): Explicitly targeting 36–37.5°C post-ROSC, actively avoiding hyperthermia (which worsens neurological outcome).
  • Metabolic Correction: Verbalising the full neuroprotection bundle: normothermia, normocapnia, normotension (MAP >60), and osmotic therapy.
Tier 4Crisis Resource Management (CRM) & Behavioural Imperatives▼

Scored in the Crisis Resource Management / Teamwork domain, and heavily weighted in the Finals.

1. Leadership & Delegation
  • Role Assignment: Identifying a leader within the first 60 seconds; delegating patient-specific roles (e.g., "you are on the airway, you are on the drugs, you are documenting").
  • Hands-off Overview: The leader does not become task-fixed (must not tunnel-vision on a single procedure/patient while losing the broader picture). Maintaining a "helicopter view".
2. Closed-Loop & Shared Mental Model Communication
  • Closed-Loop: Every instruction must be acknowledged and confirmed (e.g., "100mg given, confirm").
  • Verbalising Priorities: Maintaining a shared mental model aloud — stating the current problem, the plan, and contingency plans for the entire team to hear. Using "read-backs" for critical values.
3. Graded Assertiveness & Speaking Up
  • The Ladder of Ascendancy:
    • Level 0: Silent compliance.
    • Level 1: Hinting ("Are you sure?").
    • Level 2: Concern + Reason ("I'm worried about X because Y").
    • Level 3: Challenge + Alternative ("Stop, let's do Z instead").
    • Level 4: Physical action / escalation (stopping a colleague from carrying out a harmful order).
  • Two-Challenge Rule: An unacknowledged safety concern must be restated and escalated.
4. Dual-Patient / Resource Allocation
  • Parallel Processing: Conducting rapid ABCD assessments on multiple patients when required.
  • Calibration: Making explicit, reasoned decisions regarding shared resources — a single scanner, ultrasound machine, or blood product.
5. Structured Handover & Family Communication
  • SBAR: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation — delivered to receiving teams (PICU, Surgery, Cardiology, Burns, etc.).
  • Parent Interaction: Communicating with empathy, pacing, no jargon, and without abandoning the patient to talk; explicitly navigating difficult conversations (e.g., treatment refusal, breaking bad news, or explaining high-risk procedures).
Tier 5Additional Broad Topics for Self-Study▼

Not necessarily featured in this year's cases, but essential background knowledge for any pediatric emergency competitor.

  • Toxicology: Specific antidotes (Naloxone, Flumazenil, Hydroxocobalamin, Fomepizole) and toxidromes (cholinergic, anticholinergic, sympathomimetic).
  • Congenital Heart Disease (CHD): Duct-dependent lesions (e.g., HLHS, CoA) — recognising shock from a closing ductus and the role of Prostaglandin E1 (PGE1).
  • Metabolic / Inborn Errors of Metabolism (IEM): Unexplained severe metabolic acidosis, hyperammonaemia; initial management (stop feeds, glucose infusion, haemodialysis).
  • Haematological Crises: Sickle Cell Acute Chest Syndrome (exchange transfusion); management of severe thrombocytopenia and Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC).
  • Burns & Inhalation Injury: Parkland formula calculation for fluid resuscitation; identifying airway burns and the need for early, secured intubation.

1. Debrief Session

A short, structured debrief lasting up to 5 minutes will follow each scenario. Led by Dr. Mike Shepherd, this session is designed to encourage audience engagement by highlighting only the positive aspects of the team's performance. Constructive feedback is reserved for the written judge's sheets; the public debrief remains a celebration of effective teamwork and clinical excellence.

2. Scenario Overview

Scenarios are grounded in the emergency stabilisation of sick children and foundational PICU care. Each team has complete autonomy to choose their Team Lead, assign roles, and divide resources as they see fit.

  • Confederates: 1–2 nurse confederates from the organising team will be present in the room to assist and standardise the flow.
  • Monitoring: Relevant vitals and investigations will be displayed in real-time as teams connect the appropriate equipment.
  • Fidelity: High-fidelity manikins are used to replicate realistic physiological responses.

4. Team Assessment & Scoring (Round Outline)

Preliminary Rounds: Single clinical judge (Domain I – Clinical Judgement) and single non-clinical judge (Domain II – Crisis Resource Management). No averaging; totals are added. Domain III (Global Entrustment) is scored collectively.

Semi-Finals: Three clinical judges score Domain I independently — the domain mark is the mean of all three. Two non-clinical judges score Domain II — the mean of the two. Never the sum. Dual-patient scenarios test prioritisation and resource allocation.

Finals: Both finalists play a single case, scored directly out of 200 — Clinical Management (90 pts), Teamwork & Followership (80 pts), and Shared Reasoning (30 pts). Penalties apply for critical deviations.

Scoring Rubric Framework — how every item is marked

Every scored item on every sheet uses a fixed behavioural anchor shown to the judge inline. Case-specific items stay sealed until round start; the framework and anchors below are what every item follows.

MarkAnchor
0Absent, unsafe, or not verbalised
1Partial, delayed, or prompted
2Timely, complete, and clearly communicated

Domain I — Clinical Judgement scores what you decide: assessment and prioritisation, recognising the evolving physiology, correctly-sequenced therapies, and reassessment/disposition. Sample items: “Leads with a structured ABCDE assessment and verbalises findings” (0–2) · “Reassesses after each intervention and adapts the plan” (0–2).

Domain II — Crisis Resource Management scores how you work, across five categories: leadership & role clarity, closed-loop communication, situational awareness, resource & workload management, and decision-making. Items marked ◊ remind the judge to score the behaviour, not the diagnosis — nothing is double-counted against Domain I. Sample items: “Leader allocates roles by name within the first minute” (0–2) · “Orders are repeated back and completion is confirmed aloud” (0–2).

Domain III — Global Entrustment is a single collective 0–10 panel answer to one question: would the judges confidently entrust this team with this child in their own emergency department? Each judge first notes a private rating, then the panel agrees one mark.

Penalties subtract from a domain total for critical safety deviations (for example, missed C-spine precautions or entering a rescue state). Tiebreaks use a pre-declared question answered by the team leader alone in 60 seconds, scored against a fixed credit rubric.

7. Quick Do's & Don'ts & Myths

Event Day Conduct

✅ Do

  • Arrive at least 30 minutes before your slot with your full team present.
  • Review the published judging rubric and domain weights before you compete.
  • Practice closed-loop communication and clear role delegation beforehand.
  • Direct logistics questions to the Chief/MC — not case content questions.
  • Respect the 15-minute scenario clock and any hard stops called by the Chief.
  • Submit your team intake (roster + intro video) well before the deadline.

❌ Don't

  • Discuss case content with teams who haven't yet competed.
  • Bring personal notes, reference cards, or phones into the scenario room.
  • Exceed your registered team size or swap members without prior approval.
  • Contact judges directly to dispute a score — raise it through your team leader and the coordinators.
  • Leave the venue during your competition window without informing your coordinator.

✅ In the Scenario — Do's & ❌ Don'ts

Do Don’t
Verbalise your reasoning, priorities, and shared mental model aloud – judges can only score what they hear. Assume the judge saw what you did – if you didn’t say it or do it in the room, it didn’t happen.
Assign a clear leader and delegate specific roles within the first 60 seconds. Let the leader become task‑fixated – maintain a hands‑off overview of the whole room.
Perform structured reassessments at fixed intervals – patients evolve. Rely solely on the initial examination – clinical status changes dynamically.
Escalate to specialist teams (surgery, PICU, neurosurgery, cardiology) proactively – before catastrophic deterioration. Wait for confirmatory imaging (e.g., X‑ray) before intervening in a crashing patient – act on clinical signs.
Communicate with the parent actor – with empathy, pacing, and no jargon. Abandon the patient to talk to the parent – integrate family communication into the workflow.
Read actual documents (ECG strips, CT reports, lab values) directly. Accept verbal summaries without verifying the source document.
Accept the Stage 5 penalty calmly and continue performing to your best ability. Panic or give up if you enter Stage 5 – you can still score well in other items.
Use closed-loop communication for every critical instruction – confirm it was heard and understood. Assume a task is done without verbal confirmation – “read‑back” is essential.
Speak up assertively if you see a safety concern – use a reason + alternative (Level 2+ on the ladder). Stay silent, hint softly (Level 1), or become confrontational/aggressive.
Manage time efficiently – 15 minutes is a hard stop. Get bogged down in a single task and run out of time for reassessment and disposition.
Demonstrate the actions you want to check (e.g., abdominal exam, pupil check) – don’t just ask, PLEASE DO! Only ask for a finding without physically performing or verbalising the examination.

🧠 Myths vs. Facts

Myth Fact
You must prevent the scripted acute event (e.g., at 5:30) to score high. The event is unpreventable. You are scored on your response from where you are – branch neutrality applies.
In the semi‑finals, you add the two judges’ scores together. You average them (mean). The domain mark is out of 40 or 50, not 80 or 100.
Safety flags automatically lower your score or cause failure. Safety flags are not automatic – they inform debrief and advancement discussions but do not directly change marks.
Only the team leader is scored on speaking up. The Speaking‑Up item is weighted toward non‑leading doctors and the nurse – it measures assertiveness across the whole hierarchy.
You should stop CPR to check pupils or do a detailed examination. Minimise CPR interruptions. In certain conditions (e.g., hypothermia), fixed pupils do not indicate death – continue resuscitation.
Being loud and aggressive is the best way to score high on communication. Content over tone – a quiet, reasoned challenge scores higher than a loud but unreasoned one.
A “physiologically difficult airway” means the patient has difficult anatomy. It refers to zero physiological reserve – the patient will crash with any induction agent unless resuscitated first.
You must use the same RSI agent for every patient. Agent selection depends on underlying pathophysiology – e.g., Ketamine for asthma/shock, avoid Propofol in shock, avoid Succinylcholine in hyperkalaemia.
Hitting Stage 5 means you have lost the case. Stage 5 incurs a fixed penalty (−10), but you can still earn substantial points in other items – keep going.
The audience can use mobile phones to record and learn. Mobile phones are strictly prohibited – they must be submitted at the entrance and collected at the exit.

Good luck to all teams · Stay calm, communicate clearly, and put the patient first.
